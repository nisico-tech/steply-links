A visual bedtime routine for an autistic child

By the Steply team · 2026-11-04 · Not medical advice

A visual bedtime routine shows your child the same calm steps in the same order every night, ending in sleep: for example bath, pajamas, brush teeth, story, teddy, lights out. Use one clear picture per step, go through it together, and keep the last steps quiet and the same every night, so your child always knows what comes next and when the day is over.

Predictability matters here. The UK's NICE guideline on autism in under-19s recommends "increasing structure and minimising unpredictability", and lists changes to routines and the absence of predictability among the factors that can lead to behavior that challenges. A bedtime picture routine is a simple way to add that structure at the end of the day.

An example bedtime routine

StepWhy it is here
1. Bath or washA clear signal that the evening has started.
2. PajamasSplit it into top and bottom if needed.
3. Brush teethSame picture as in the morning routine.
4. ToiletLast stop before bed.
5. StoryThe calm reward: the same length every night.
6. Teddy and lights outA clear end. Nothing comes after this card.
Put the thing your child enjoys most (often the story) near the end, so the routine always moves toward something good.

Make the end obvious

A schedule shows a sequence and when it is finished. The AFIRM visual supports module describes routine schedules as the steps of one routine, such as bedtime. The last card matters most: make it the same every night ("lights out" with a picture of their own bed or teddy), and do not add steps after it. In Steply, the routine ends with a calm All done screen.

Keep the screen calm, or use paper

If you use a phone or tablet at bedtime, keep it simple and short. Many families prefer a printed strip on the bedroom door for the last steps and keep the phone for the bath and pajamas part, or for nights away from home. There is no single right format: in a study comparing book and tablet schedules, children learned at similar speeds with both, and preferences varied from child to child. You can print our free bedtime routine strip.

When bedtime goes wrong

  • A step is refused every night: it may be too big or too unpleasant. Split it, shorten it, or move it earlier in the evening.
  • Bedtime is late tonight: keep the same steps, just shorter. Show your child a shorter story card instead of skipping the story without warning.
  • Away from home: bring the same pictures. The same order in a new place helps the day end the usual way.
  • A hard moment: stop the routine, help your child calm down first, then show the next card again. A slow breathing circle together can help some children; Steply's Help now has one.

Sleep problems are worth raising

If your child often takes a very long time to fall asleep, wakes for long periods at night, or is very tired in the day, talk to your child's doctor. A routine is one helpful tool; it is not a treatment for a sleep problem.

Sources

  • NICE CG170, Autism spectrum disorder in under 19s: support and management
  • AFIRM, Visual Supports brief packet, University of North Carolina
  • Book versus tablet activity schedules (PubMed 28452230)
  • NCAEP, Evidence-Based Practices report, 2020
