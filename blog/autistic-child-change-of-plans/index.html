How to help an autistic child cope with a change of plans

By the Steply team · 2026-11-02 · Not medical advice

To help an autistic child cope with a change of plans, show the change instead of only saying it: point to the old plan, show clearly what replaces it ("instead of the park, we go to the library"), keep the rest of the day the same, and put something familiar or liked straight after the change. Tell them as early as you calmly can, use the same words and pictures for changes every time, and have a ready "Plan B" for the changes you can predict, such as rain, a closed place or a sick day.

Changes are hard for many autistic children for a real reason. A 2020 review in the journal Autism found that in 9 of 10 studies with the right statistics, anxiety in autistic people was linked to intolerance of uncertainty, the difficulty of coping with the unexpected. The UK's NICE guideline lists "changes to routines" and "the absence of predictability and structure" among the factors that can lead to behavior that challenges. You cannot stop plans from changing, but you can make each change more predictable.

Why "we'll do it later" often makes things worse

When a plan changes, a child hears that something they expected is gone. If the message is vague, such as "something is different today", the uncertainty grows. What helps most is knowing exactly what happens instead, and seeing that the rest of the day is still the same.

Show the replacement, not just the loss. "Park is closed" is a loss. "Park closed. Library instead. Then home and snack" is a plan.

Step 1: Make your normal plan visible

Changes are easier to show when the usual plan is already visible as a visual schedule or a First/Then board. If you do not use one yet, start there: our guide on how to make a visual schedule walks through it. Visual supports are recognized as an evidence-based practice for autistic children by the 2020 NCAEP review.

Step 2: Show the change with "instead of"

Many families use a simple "change card" or "instead of" board:

  1. Point to the card that is changing: "Park."
  2. Cover it, cross it out, or move it aside: "No park today."
  3. Put the new card in the same place: "Library instead."
  4. Point to what stays the same after it: "Then home. Then snack."

Use the same symbol and the same words for every change, for example a "change" card with an arrow. Over time, the change card itself becomes familiar, which makes the next change a little less surprising.

Step 3: Prepare Plan B for the changes you can predict

Some changes come back again and again. Make their pictures before you need them:

Common changePlan B to have ready
Rain on a park dayIndoor play, library, bath with toys
A place is closedA second favorite place nearby
Sick day, no schoolA calm home routine: rest, drink, a favorite show
A different adult picks upA photo of who is coming, and where they will meet
Running lateA shorter version of the routine with fewer steps

Practicing a Plan B on a calm day, when nothing has gone wrong, helps your child learn it before the stressful moment comes.

Step 4: Think about timing

Telling a child about a change early sounds always helpful, but the research is mixed. In small studies with preschool children, a two-minute warning before a transition and advance notice on its own did not reduce problems; other approaches were needed. Practice guidelines on advance notice explain why: warnings tend to help when the upset comes from not knowing what happens next, and help less when the upset comes from having to stop something enjoyable.

  • If your child gets upset by not knowing, show the change early and clearly.
  • If your child gets upset by stopping something they love, show what good thing comes next, and keep the gap short.
  • If a countdown or timer makes your child more anxious, it is fine to stop using it.

Step 5: Keep everything else the same

On a day with one change, try not to change other things too. Keep the same words, the same order for the rest of the routine, and familiar comfort items close. A child who has already handled one surprise has less room left for a second.

Step 6: When the whole day falls apart

Some days nothing goes to plan. It is fine to stop, drop the schedule to the next one or two steps, and start the day again from a calm point. A shorter, quieter plan for the rest of the day is still a plan, and your child can still see what comes next.

When to get extra help

If changes often lead to long meltdowns, self-injury or very high anxiety, or if your child's distress is growing, speak with your child's doctor, an occupational therapist, a psychologist or a behavior specialist. They can look at what drives the distress for your child and build a plan that fits. Visual supports can be part of that plan, but they are not a treatment on their own.

How Steply helps

Steply was designed around this moment. When the plan changes, you can swap one card for another and your child sees the new card in the same place, show a single "Now" card, or pick a ready Plan B such as "Rainy day" or "Place is closed". If the whole day goes wrong, "Reset the day" starts again calmly without losing your routines. It is a tool for showing the plan, not therapy, and it does not promise fewer meltdowns.
