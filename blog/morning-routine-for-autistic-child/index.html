A morning routine for an autistic child, picture by picture

By the Steply team · 2026-11-04 · Not medical advice

A good visual morning routine for a young autistic child has 4 to 6 steps, one clear picture per step, the same order every school day, and something your child likes at the end. Show it before you start, go through it together one step at a time, and let your child mark each step as done. Split any step that keeps going wrong into smaller ones.

Mornings are often the hardest part of the day: there is a deadline, many small steps, and little room for surprises. Visual supports are one of the best-studied tools for exactly this. The 2020 NCAEP review lists visual supports as an evidence-based practice for autistic children, and the UK's NICE guideline recommends visual supports that are meaningful for the child.

An example morning, step by step

StepPictureTip
1. ToiletYour own toilet, or a simple toilet pictureStart with something quick and easy, so the first Done comes fast.
2. Brush teethYour child's own toothbrushA photo of the real toothbrush is often clearer than an icon.
3. Get dressedToday's clothes, laid outIf this step stalls, split it: pants, shirt, left sock, right sock.
4. BreakfastTheir bowl or plateKeep the same picture every day, even if the food changes.
5. BackpackTheir backpack by the doorPack it the evening before, so this step is just "pick it up".
6. Shoes and coatTheir shoesEnd at the door, ready to go.
Five or six steps is plenty for most young children. If your child is new to schedules, start with three: toilet, brush teeth, shoes.

Choose pictures your child recognizes

Use the kind of picture your child understands best. For young or minimally verbal children, realistic color photos are often clearest: in a 2015 study, minimally verbal autistic children could use all the picture types tested but did best with color photographs. Add a short word under each picture, even if your child does not read yet, and keep the same picture for the same step every day.

Split the step that keeps going wrong

Most mornings stall on one step, often getting dressed. That usually means the step is too big. Break it into the smallest actions your child can see: "left sock", "right sock" instead of "get dressed". In Steply, a step can hold small steps, so your child sees "Left sock" and then "Right sock" as two big pictures, each with its own Done.

Teach it, then step back

Children usually need to be shown how a schedule works. In a classic study of photographic activity schedules, autistic children were first guided by hand and the guidance was gradually faded, and they went on to follow their schedules on their own. At home that can look like this:

  • First week: you point to each picture, say the word, help with the step, and help mark it done.
  • Next: point, say the word, and wait a few seconds before helping.
  • Then: just point. Later, your child checks the schedule without you.

When the morning goes off plan

Something will change: no clean socks, a late bus, a sick sibling. Many autistic children find the unexpected hard; a 2020 review found a consistent link between anxiety and difficulty tolerating uncertainty in autistic people. Show the change on the schedule instead of only saying it. Swap the card, or show a simple First / Then: "first shoes, then car". Keep your voice calm and your words short.

What the research can and cannot promise

Visual schedules help many children, but the studies are mostly small. A 2015 review of visual activity schedules found most good-quality studies showed them to be effective, but nearly all were single-case designs. Treat a morning schedule as one helpful tool, used alongside advice from your child's therapist or teacher, and adjust it to your child.
